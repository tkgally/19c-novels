His father offered to bring him into parliament; but he had no taste for London, and declined the offer in favour of a younger brother, who was destined for the bar, and likely to distinguish himself at it. Mr. Sedley promised to be, that now rare character, an <i>English country gentleman</i>. He was happy, because he was <i>useful</i>, and for that reason he was respected and beloved.

But Frederic despised him, said he did not know the world, <i>i. e.</i> he did not know the <i>town</i>, the luxuries and vicious haunts of London and Paris, the pernicious scandal of their clubs and saloons, or the idle trifling of Brighton. Poor Frederic Vernon fell into a usual error. He thought <i>his little set</i> mankind. Yet it galled him to see his neighbour more cheerful, more self-possessed, than himself, without reflecting that this sort of satisfaction arose wholly from the consciousness of doing his duty by <i>others</i>; not indulging <i>his own</i> exclusive and selfish gratification. By making himself <i>useful</i>, he had an applauding conscience. Had Frederic such?
