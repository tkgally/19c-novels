She was an orphan with a small portion, and could she have been contented to live in her native province of Munster, that portion would have maintained her in comfort and respectability, and even allowed of her visiting the capital, and enjoying occasionally the gaities of the Phœnix-park, but, after the Union had diminished the splendour of the Irish capital, Miss O’Brian <i>must</i> pass her springs in London, where <i>all</i> her friends were.

Somebody has remarked, “that few people suffer more privations than the <i>shabby genteel</i>,” those who make appearances above their means; and however Miss O’Brian might, with all her line of one hundred and ninety-seven kings, be offended at being so classed, yet a very small lodging in Green-street, Grosvenor-square, one extraordinary Irish maid, who lived with her more from family attachment than emolument, was her whole establishment; her anxiety to be asked <i>every where</i>, drove her to various shifts that might be called degrading; but to get <i>taken</i> where she was invited was a work of still greater difficulty; to prevail on one friend to take her to, on another to bring her from, would, to a
