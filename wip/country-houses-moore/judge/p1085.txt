[[cont]]and if an Indian judgeship, where the incumbent could not expect to live seven years, has had forty-seven candidates, this <i>matrimonial</i> Chancellor’s situation, whose life would have nothing beyond the common risk, would be competed for by all the silk gowns at the bar. Curious speculations would arise from such a new court; and even the clubs in St. James’-street and Pall-mall would be furnished with subjects for betting; and what anxiety would be created among portionless beauties as to <i>who</i> this powerful Chancellor would decree to <i>them</i>. He would be more sought after, more flattered, by Lady Mothers and their charming daughters, than any lord who ever sat on a woolsack—and his wife! if he had one, how she would be invited, and fêted!—But we must leave these speculations to future consideration, and return to Lady Honiton, who felt most acutely, both in body and mind, that this was her third season, and no daughter married; her Lord, unfortunately, did not enter into her feelings, indeed men could not understand such delicacy.

She was fortunate in having a friend, who, if she was not duly qualified to understand <i>all</i> she felt, yet was a patient listener, and a kind and soothing sympathizer.
