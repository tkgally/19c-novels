[[cont]]where there was the choicest music, both vocal and instrumental; all the first professors, and the most celebrated singers; during this the gardens were illuminated by myriads of fantastic lights, and fire-works were displayed on the water; it was a scene of enchantment, calculated to fit the heart for tender impressions; but Mrs. Beaumont, and Miss Talbot, were doomed to listen to the long dull <i>tirades</i> of Mon. Le Fronde, who, in his own opinion, was <i>un bon raconteur</i>, in that of every other person, a <i>dull</i>, <i>prosing bore</i>; but Emily was amused, the Count exerted himself to the utmost, and often with a shrug and sly look toward M. Le Fronde, testified his satisfaction at the change fortune had on that occasion allotted <i>him</i>.

When the three ladies were seated in Mrs. Beaumont’s carriage, Miss Talbot said,

“I think, Emily, you must have had a delightful evening, and you seemed fully to enjoy it.”

“Giddy things of her age,” replied Mrs. Beaumont, “are easily pleased, or I am sure Emily could not have been so to night, for, to my mind, it was as dull a thing as I ever wish to partake of.”

Emily declared herself <i>charmed</i> with the whole thing; she only wished dancing had been added to make it complete.
