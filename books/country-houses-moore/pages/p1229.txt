Lord Honiton had regretted (though he had not said so,) that his house would be full of company on a Sunday; but it would not interrupt his course of duty, and he hoped but little that of his servants;—though he felt he had no right to compel his guests to the same observance, and he dreaded the force of example which the careless and dissolute domestics of the people of the world might spread amongst his sober and steady dependants. The laws of society keep persons in the higher grades from broaching opinions not in unison with those of their entertainers, but in the lower departments of a large establishment there is no such restraint, and incalculable mischief is done by one profligate servant belonging to an accidental visitor.

Lord Honiton’s own steady course nothing interrupted, he served his God first, and then did, to the best of his power, his duty to his neighbours; but though he did this in a quiet, unoffending way, he did it with a steadiness that was imposing, and awed even the levity of those of different habits.
