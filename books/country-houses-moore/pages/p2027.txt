The courier who conducted the foreigners, departed the morning after his arrival; he had a short conference previously with Lord Dunster; but as he professed to understand only German and French, no information could be elicited from him by the <i>domestics</i>.

The young Comtesse had been used to seclusion; she lived chiefly in her own apartment; she was rich in resources; and the Chanoinesse, who was something between a <i>bonne</i>, a governess, and a chaprone, was always cheerful, and soon made herself a favourite throughout the house. Not so the priest, Mr. Meynel, as Lord Dunster called him, though he was generally designated “<i>the father</i>” in the family, a <i>soi-disant</i> title. His position was rather equivocal; by Lord Dunster he was treated haughtily, and quite as an inferior and dependent, and yet there was a sort of indefinable insolence in his manner towards his apparent patron, that said, “Offend me if you dare!”

Lady Gertrude always avoided him, she could not endure his peremptory and supercilious manner. The housekeeper disliked him for his fastidiousness on the subject of cookery, and the housemaids detested him for the dirtiness of his apartment; over and above all, good Mrs. Bartlet was <i>sure</i>, and repeated the certainty on every occasion, “that there would be no luck in the house whilst those papists staid.”
