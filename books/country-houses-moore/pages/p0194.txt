Emma was accustomed to no such indulgence, and to no such fine ladyism; she laughed at the idea, and said:—

“A little dance with Lady Tintern’s children, who were in the room, would do her much more good; that really the Count and Mr. Fitz-Gibbon had both been so good natured and encouraging, she had lost a great many of her fears.”

“Oh, my dear, you say so now; people, even those as temperate as you are, always feel stronger after dinner, but you do not know how you will feel when you see a large audience listening to you.”

“Mr. Fitz Gibbon,” replied Miss Legh very modestly “has instructed me not to look at all at the audience; only to fancy them sofas and chairs; and to see, if I can, those only who are on the stage: I shall try to remember his caution.”

“Mr. Fitz Gibbon is a very good instructor,” said Mrs. De Cameron, “but one cannot fight against nature, and I am sure your’s is a very timid one.”
