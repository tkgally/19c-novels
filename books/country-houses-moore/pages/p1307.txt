“But what pledge can you fear in the other house? Alas! that of voting against Catholic emancipation is at an end. I should be sorry we differed on that, or any other point; but <i>that</i> is ceded now.”

“Only in part,” replied Lord Ottery, “no liberal or enlightened person can suppose that those long withheld <i>just</i> rights, which have been now so tardily and reluctantly granted to the Catholics as a <i>boon</i>, will satisfy them. No! they will <i>claim</i>, and I trust <i>obtain</i> the restitution of <i>every</i> right and privilege their forefathers possessed, and which the Protestants have so unjustly withheld.”

When this conversation began, Lord Honiton was standing with his back to the grate, though there was no fire, and swaying, first on one foot and then on the other; but at this speech he raised himself bolt upright, fixed his eyes stedfastly on the door opposite to him, and remained for a few minutes as if turned to stone, for his face was as pale as death, his son looked anxiously at him, there were icy fingers on his heart; but his blood ran cold only for a few seconds, when the warm tide rushed with double force to its usual course;
