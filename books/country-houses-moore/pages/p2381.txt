Unfortunately, commerce with the world seldom <i>cures</i> defects of the <i>heart</i>; though it may instruct how to <i>hide</i> such as are offensive or discreditable.

It is only that B<sc>eing</sc> “who knoweth whereof we are made,” and sees the inmost recesses of the soul—the thoughts and imaginations, whence all our actions spring—who has power to root out <i>vanity</i>, or any of our evil propensities.

One of the most powerful impulses, by which our <i>spiritual enemy</i> works, is our <i>vanity</i>. Often, when <i>he</i> cannot prevent us from doing well, he can deprive us of the benefit of a good action by making us vain of it. And numerous are the occasions on which, if we dared to trust ourselves to examine the motives of our actions, we should discover that their most secret spring was that <i>vanity</i> which would destroy <i>our own</i> good opinion of them, and make us ashamed of what we had before valued ourselves on.

But desultory observations will not bring the truth home to our own hearts; we must examine in what instances <i>vanity</i> is most likely to govern our actions.

“<i>There be many things that increase vanity.</i>”

Perhaps, amongst women, none more than personal beauty, on which they are from infancy taught to set too high a value.
