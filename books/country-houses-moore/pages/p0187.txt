Mrs. De Cameron and Mr. Fitz Gibbon were at chess, and amused themselves with this scene; the former ridiculed the idea of such a raw girl being able to play the part assigned her, and prognosticated a failure, and the consequent discomfort of Lady Tintern which she said “would serve her right for rejecting <i>her</i> offer.” Much as Fitz Gibbon admired the bloom and natural charms of Emma, he curled his lip a little contemptuously at a want of tact, in <i>La belle provinciale</i>, as he called her, and prophesied that Dunois’ encomiums would rather overpower, than encourage her.

He was mistaken: the following day after breakfast a rehearsal was proposed. Emma was perfect in her part; she learnt it with a school girl’s facility, having risen early for the purpose. Fitz Gibbon then saw there was capability about her, and encouraged her; but the Count’s flattery and applause so distressed her, added to the exertion, that at the conclusion she burst into tears, and Lady Tintern reproached herself, and was provoked with the others, for expecting too much
